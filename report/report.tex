\begin{abstract}
We study regression, classification and stock-return prediction. On the Diabetes dataset, ElasticNet and Lasso regression implemented from scratch with gradient descent reach test mean squared errors (MSE) of 2,774 and 2,804 ($R^2 = 0.49$ and $0.48$), slightly better than scikit-learn's LinearRegression (2,822), while shrinking redundant correlated features. On the Digits dataset, a one-vs-rest support vector machine (SVM) and a one-hidden-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) implemented from scratch reach test accuracies of 95.6\% and 97.0\%; scikit-learn's RandomForestClassifier also reaches 96.5\%.
In Task 3, we predict the next-day volatility-adjusted returns of S\&P~500 stocks with a Lasso, a random forest and an MLP refit on rolling three-year windows. On 2011--2013, the random forest and the MLP reach Sharpe ratios of 3.19 and 2.34, but only 1.58 and 0.77 when trades are executed one day later.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ==================================================================
This assignment applies supervised learning to three problems. Task~1 analyzes and preprocesses two benchmark datasets. The Diabetes dataset contains 10 baseline variables (age, sex, body mass index, blood pressure and six blood-serum measurements $s_1$--$s_6$) for 442 patients, with a continuous measure of disease progression one year later as the target. The Digits dataset contains 1,797 grayscale $8\times8$ images of handwritten digits, giving 64 pixel features with integer intensities from 0 to 16 and 10 classes. Task~2 implements four models from scratch with gradient descent, using only NumPy: ElasticNet and Lasso regression on Diabetes, and a multiclass SVM and an MLP on Digits. They are compared with scikit-learn's LinearRegression and RandomForestClassifier~\cite{sklearn}, using a 70\%/30\% train/test split with random state 42 (stratified by class for Digits). Regression is evaluated with the mean squared error (MSE) and the coefficient of determination ($R^2$), and classification with accuracy.

Task~3 uses daily returns of S\&P~500 constituents from 1980 to 2013 and social-media sentiment scores to predict which stocks will outperform on the next trading day, judging a Lasso, a random forest and an MLP by the Sharpe ratio of the portfolio they define. We find that regularization slightly improves on ordinary least squares on Diabetes by removing redundant correlated features, that the MLP and the random forest are equally accurate on Digits, ahead of a linear SVM, and that past returns carry small but persistent information about next-day relative returns, best captured by a random forest, although much of it requires trading at the closing prices used to form the predictions.

\section{Task 2: Models Implemented from Scratch}
\label{sec:task2}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{Methodology}

\textit{Evaluation.} Both datasets are split into 70\% training and 30\% test data with random state 42; the Digits split is additionally stratified by class. For $n$ test samples with targets $y_i$ and predictions $\hat y_i$, regression is evaluated with
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2, \quad
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i}(y_i-\bar{y})^2},
\end{equation}
where $\bar y$ is the mean test target. $R^2$ compares squared prediction error with the error of predicting that mean and can be negative. Classification is evaluated with accuracy, the fraction of test samples classified correctly. For the regression models, each hyperparameter was varied one at a time around a fixed baseline, using test MSE to describe sensitivity; the final models retain the baseline settings. For the classifiers, grid search uses mean accuracy over five stratified cross-validation folds without shuffling, constructed from the training set. Separate training-duration sweeps are diagnostic; the final SVM and MLP retain the 400 iterations and 400 epochs used in their initial grids.

\subsubsection{ElasticNet Regression}
For weights $\mathbf w$, bias $b$ and $N$ training pairs $(\mathbf x_j, y_j)$, ElasticNet~\cite{zou2005} minimizes
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L(\mathbf w, b) = \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bigl(y_j - \mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_j - b\bigr)^2 + \lambda_1\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_1 + \frac{\lambda_2}{2}\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_2^2,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_1$ controls the L1 penalty, which drives weights to zero and so selects features, and $\lambda_2$ the L2 penalty, which shrinks weights and spreads them across correlated features. We set $\lambda_1 = \alpha\rho$ and $\lambda_2 = \alpha(1-\rho)$, with overall strength $\alpha$ and L1 ratio $\rho$. With predictions $\hat{\mathbf y} = X\mathbf w + b$ and learning rate $\eta$, gradient descent updates
\begin{gather}
\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w - \eta\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{N}X^\top(\hat{\mathbf y}-\mathbf y) + \lambda_1\,\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf w) + \lambda_2\mathbf w\Bigr], \nonumber\\
b \leftarrow b - \frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\hat y_j - y_j),
\end{gather}
using the subgradient $\mathrm{sign}(w_k)$ of $|w_k|$, with $\mathrm{sign}(0) = 0$.

\subsubsection{Lasso Regression with Soft Thresholding}
The Lasso~\cite{tibshirani1996} is the special case $\lambda_2 = 0$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf w, b) = \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bigl(y_j - \mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_j - b\bigr)^2 + \lambda_1\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_1 .
\end{equation}
Instead of the subgradient of $|w_k|$, which makes weights oscillate around zero, each iteration takes a gradient step on the squared error and then applies the soft-thresholding operator, the proximal operator of the L1 penalty:
\begin{gather}
\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathcal S\Bigl(\mathbf w - \tfrac{\eta}{N}X^\top(\hat{\mathbf y}-\mathbf y),\ \eta\lambda_1\Bigr), \nonumber\\
\mathcal{S}(x, \gamma) = \mathrm{sign}(x)\max(|x| - \gamma, 0),
\end{gather}
applied elementwise, with the same bias update as ElasticNet. Weights whose magnitude falls below the threshold $\gamma = \eta\lambda_1$ are set exactly to zero, which produces sparse solutions.

\subsubsection{Linear Regression}
Scikit-learn's LinearRegression fits ordinary least squares, minimizing $\lVert \mathbf y-X\mathbf w-b\rVert_2^2$ without a penalty. It solves the least-squares problem using a numerically stable solver rather than gradient descent. For centred data, when $X^\top X$ is invertible, the solution can be written as $\mathbf w=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y$; this expression describes the solution rather than the numerical procedure used by the implementation. LinearRegression serves as the unregularized reference for ElasticNet and the Lasso.

\subsubsection{Multiclass SVM}
For a binary problem with labels $t_j \in \{-1, +1\}$, a linear SVM~\cite{cortes1995} predicts with $f(\mathbf x) = \mathbf w^\top\mathbf x + b$. It is trained by minimizing the L2-regularized hinge loss
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L = \frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_2^2 + \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\max\bigl(0,\, 1 - t_j f(\mathbf x_j)\bigr),
\label{eq:hinge}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the regularization parameter. The hinge loss is not differentiable where $t_j f(\mathbf x_j) = 1$, and we take its subgradient to be zero there. With $\mathcal S = \{j : t_j f(\mathbf x_j) < 1\}$, the set of samples that are misclassified or inside the margin, the subgradients of~\eqref{eq:hinge} are
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\mathbf w}\mathcal L = \lambda\mathbf w - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in\mathcal S} t_j\mathbf x_j, \qquad
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial b} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in\mathcal S} t_j .
\end{equation}
Starting from $\mathbf w = \mathbf 0$ and $b = 0$, subgradient descent with learning rate $\eta$ repeats $\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w - \eta\nabla_{\mathbf w}\mathcal L$ and $b \leftarrow b - \eta\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial b$ for a fixed number of iterations, using all training samples at each step. For the ten digit classes, we use one-vs-rest (OvR): for each class $k \in \{0, \ldots, 9\}$, a binary SVM $f_k$ is trained with $t_j = 1$ if $y_j = k$ and $t_j = -1$ otherwise, and a sample is assigned to the class with the highest score, $\hat y = \argmax_k f_k(\mathbf x)$. The hyperparameters are $\lambda$, $\eta$ and the number of iterations.

\subsubsection{Multilayer Perceptron}
The MLP has $n_0 = 64$ input units, one hidden layer of $n_1$ units with the rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation, $\mathrm{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$, and $n_2 = 10$ softmax output units. Its parameters are the weight matrices $W^{(1)} \in \mathbb R^{n_1\times n_0}$ and $W^{(2)} \in \mathbb R^{n_2\times n_1}$ and the bias vectors $\mathbf b^{(1)} \in \mathbb R^{n_1}$ and $\mathbf b^{(2)} \in \mathbb R^{n_2}$. For an input $\mathbf x$, the forward pass computes
\begin{gather}
\mathbf z^{(1)} = W^{(1)}\mathbf x + \mathbf b^{(1)}, \qquad \mathbf a^{(1)} = \mathrm{ReLU}\bigl(\mathbf z^{(1)}\bigr), \nonumber\\
f(\mathbf x) = \sigma\bigl(W^{(2)}\mathbf a^{(1)} + \mathbf b^{(2)}\bigr),
\end{gather}
where ReLU is applied elementwise and $\sigma$ is the softmax, $\sigma(\mathbf z)_c = e^{z_c}/\sum_{k} e^{z_k}$, so that $f(\mathbf x)_c$ is the predicted probability of class $c$. With $\tilde{\mathbf y}_j$ the one-hot encoding of the class $y_j$ of sample $\mathbf x_j$, the model minimizes the cross-entropy loss
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{c=1}^{n_2}(\tilde{\mathbf y}_j)_c\log f(\mathbf x_j)_c = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log f(\mathbf x_j)_{y_j}.
\end{equation}
For softmax with cross-entropy, the gradient with respect to the output pre-activation of sample $j$ simplifies to $\boldsymbol\delta^{(2)}_j = f(\mathbf x_j) - \tilde{\mathbf y}_j$. Backpropagation then gives
\begin{gather*}
\nabla_{W^{(2)}}\mathcal L = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\boldsymbol\delta^{(2)}_j\,\mathbf a^{(1)\top}_j, \qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf b^{(2)}}\mathcal L = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\boldsymbol\delta^{(2)}_j,\\
\boldsymbol\delta^{(1)}_j = \bigl(W^{(2)\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(2)}_j\bigr)\odot\mathrm{ReLU}'\bigl(\mathbf z^{(1)}_j\bigr),\\
\nabla_{W^{(1)}}\mathcal L = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\boldsymbol\delta^{(1)}_j\,\mathbf x_j^\top, \qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf b^{(1)}}\mathcal L = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\boldsymbol\delta^{(1)}_j,
\end{gather*}
where $\odot$ is the elementwise product and $\mathrm{ReLU}'(z) = 1$ if $z > 0$ and $0$ otherwise. Weights are initialized from a normal distribution with standard deviation 0.001 (random seed 42) to break the symmetry between hidden units, and biases at zero. Each epoch performs one full-batch gradient-descent step $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\nabla_\theta\mathcal L$ on every parameter $\theta$, with learning rate $\eta$. The hyperparameters are the hidden size $n_1$, $\eta$ and the number of epochs.

\subsubsection{Random Forest}
A random forest~\cite{breiman2001} is an ensemble of decision trees whose prediction is the majority vote of the trees. Each tree is grown on a bootstrap sample of the training set, drawn with replacement, and only a random subset of the features is considered at each split, which decorrelates the trees. At each node, the tree chooses the split that minimizes the weighted Gini impurity of the two children,
\begin{equation}
G_{\text{split}} = \frac{n_L}{n}G_L + \frac{n_R}{n}G_R, \qquad G = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K}p_k^2,
\end{equation}
where $p_k$ is the proportion of class $k$ in a node, $n_L$ and $n_R$ are the numbers of samples in the left and right children, and $n = n_L + n_R$. Splitting continues until a node is pure or the maximum depth is reached. The hyperparameters tuned are the number of trees and the maximum depth.

\subsection{Results}

\subsubsection{Regression}
With learning rate $\eta=0.05$, 10,000 iterations, $\alpha=0.05$ and $\rho=0.5$ (so $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=0.025$), on standardized features and target, our ElasticNet reaches a test MSE of 2773.64 ($R^2=0.4862$), against 2821.75 ($R^2=0.4773$) for LinearRegression. The Lasso, with $\lambda_1=0.05$, reaches 2803.57 ($R^2=0.4807$). Both regularized models slightly improve on ordinary least squares on this split. Predicted-versus-actual and residual plots are provided in the notebook.

The coefficients differ in an informative way (Fig.~\ref{fig:regression_coefficients}). LinearRegression gives large opposite weights to the strongly correlated cholesterol measures $s_1$ ($-0.55$) and $s_2$ ($+0.31$), illustrating how correlated predictors can receive offsetting coefficients. ElasticNet shrinks these coefficients to approximately $-0.034$ and $-0.001$, respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:regression_coefficients}(a)). The Lasso sets the coefficients of age, $s_1$, $s_2$, $s_4$ and $s_6$ exactly to zero (Fig.~\ref{fig:regression_coefficients}(b)). Its largest remaining coefficients in magnitude are those of body mass index, $s_5$, blood pressure and $s_3$, with a smaller negative coefficient for sex. Unlike the proximal Lasso, the subgradient ElasticNet implementation can retain small nonzero coefficients near zero.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:t3_problem}

At the close of each trading day $t$, we predict the volatility-adjusted return $\tilde r_{t+1,i}$ (Section~\ref{sec:t3_analysis}) of every stock $i$ that is an S\&P~500 member on day $t$, using only information available at that close. The data contain daily excess returns for 1,335 securities from 1980 to 2013, daily index membership and, from July 2009, bullish and bearish sentiment scores. We use the membership list of the prediction date, so the stocks predicted are those in the index at that time, about 500 per day. Every evaluated strategy exceeds the required 100 daily predictions; the minimum number of scored stocks is 487 during development and 491 during testing. We treat the task as regression rather than classification of the return's sign, because the portfolio built from the predictions invests in proportion to the predicted value, so its magnitude matters. Outcomes from 1990 to 2010 form the development period, used for model selection, and outcomes from 2011 to 2013 form the test period; earlier data provide initial training and feature history. Predictions are judged mainly by the annualized Sharpe ratio of the resulting portfolio, supported by cumulative rank correlation, holding period, correlation with the market and sensitivity to a one-day execution delay (Section~\ref{sec:t3_metrics}). We compare the three models suggested by the assignment, a Lasso, a random forest and an MLP, with two rules that require no fitting.

\subsection{Data Analysis}
\label{sec:t3_analysis}

\textit{Data quality.} The returns and membership tables share 8,577 trading days. Twenty-one permanent identifiers (permnos) appear twice as column headers with two different return series, so we keep both as distinct securities. Twenty-one columns are entirely empty and were removed, leaving 1,314 securities. Index membership ranges from 489 to 500 stocks per day, and no member return is missing. Returns are also given for stocks on days when they are not members (2.1 million such stock-days before 2011). The supplied universe contains stocks that were members at some point between 1980 and 2013, so using all available securities as training examples could introduce bias from the retrospectively defined universe. Non-member days are therefore used only to estimate volatility and features, never as training targets, in cross-sectional rankings or in evaluation. Sentiment is sparse: in 2010, only 11.6\% of members have a usable pair of scores on a given day. All exploratory statistics use data before 2011 only.

\textit{Distributions and volatility.} Raw member returns are heavy-tailed, with an excess kurtosis of 39.3 (0 for a Gaussian), and their scale changes strongly over time: their cross-sectional standard deviation more than doubles during crises (Fig.~\ref{fig:t3_voladj}, top). Following the assignment, we estimate each stock's mean and variance with exponentially weighted moving averages (EWMA),
\begin{align}
\hat\mu_t &= (1-\beta_2)\,r_t + \beta_2\,\hat\mu_{t-1},\\
\hat\sigma^2_t &= (1-\beta_1)(r_t-\hat\mu_t)^2
                 + \beta_1\,\hat\sigma^2_{t-1},
\end{align}
and form $\tilde r_t=r_t/\hat\sigma_{t-1}$, dividing by the previous day's estimate because $\hat\sigma_t$ depends on $r_t$ itself. An observation $k$ days old has weight proportional to $\beta^k$, so the half-life is $\ln 2/(-\ln\beta)$. We use $\beta_1=0.97$, a variance half-life of about 23 trading days, so that volatility estimates follow regime changes without responding entirely to daily noise; $\beta_2=0.99$, a half-life of about 69 days, because the daily mean is small and noisy; an initial variance of $0.02^2$; and a burn-in of 100 observed returns per security, after which the direct weight on the initial variance is below 5\%. The state is carried forward unchanged on missing-return days. These settings were fixed before the compact run rather than tuned. The adjusted returns have a more stable scale, with a cross-sectional standard deviation between about 0.6 and 1.1 (Fig.~\ref{fig:t3_voladj}, bottom), although substantial dips remain around and after crises. Heavy tails remain: excess kurtosis falls to 22.5, and about 0.2\% of observed adjusted returns exceed 5 in absolute value, against approximately six in ten million for a Gaussian. This motivates clipping the fitted targets and ranking the features.

\textit{Exploratory signals and features.} Two classical effects, evaluated as rules without fitting, suggest predictive information in past returns. Short-term reversal, which predicts minus the mean clipped adjusted return of the last five days, reaches a development Sharpe ratio of 2.18, but this falls from 4.08 in 1990--1994 to 1.25 in 2005--2010 (Table~\ref{tab:t3_dev}). Twelve-month momentum is much weaker (0.52). Among 2010 members, the ranked 1-, 5- and 21-day return features are moderately correlated with one another (0.19 to 0.44), because they share recent days, while momentum and volatility have little correlation with these short-horizon features. These findings motivate a small set of return predictors and regular re-estimation on recent data.

\subsection{Data Processing and Leakage Prevention}
\label{sec:t3_processing}

\textit{Target and features.} Section~\ref{sec:t3_rationale} explains the choices below. To construct the training target of row $t$, we clip the next trading day's adjusted return to $[-5,5]$ and then demean it across the members of day $t$ with finite targets; evaluation uses the unclipped return. We use four predictors computed from clipped adjusted returns and one based on the EWMA volatility estimate, all available at the close of day $t$: the return of day $t$ (1-day reversal); its mean over days $t-4$ to $t$ (5-day reversal, at least three observations) and over $t-20$ to $t$ (21-day reversal, at least 15); 12-1 month momentum, the sum over days $t-251$ to $t-21$, requiring all 231 observations; and $\log\hat\sigma_t$, the current risk level. Each predictor is ranked among the members of day $t$ and scaled to $[-0.5,0.5]$, and missing values are set to the neutral value 0 after ranking. Mean member coverage over 1990--2010 before filling is approximately 99.77\% for the 1-day return, 99.76\% for the 5-day return, 99.73\% for the 21-day return, 98.99\% for momentum and 99.77\% for log volatility. For the sentiment comparison, we add net sentiment (bullish minus bearish) and activity (bullish plus bearish), both ranked, and a flag marking stocks with usable scores; duplicated permnos are excluded because their scores cannot be assigned unambiguously to a security.

\textit{Walk-forward refits.} Every 252 trading days, each model is refit on the latest 756 eligible feature days, using member rows with a finite target and at most 100,000 stock-days sampled without replacement. The sampling seed is $42+252j$ for refit $j=0,1,\ldots$ within each walk-forward run; forest and MLP random states are fixed at 42. Each fitted model predicts every day until the next refit. The first prediction is made on 29 December 1989 and scored on 2 January 1990. During the test period the same schedule continues, so later refits train on test-period outcomes that have already been realized, as a live strategy would; test performance never selects a configuration.

\textit{Leakage prevention.} A row enters a fit only if its label date, the next trading day, falls strictly before the refit date. The row of the trading day just before a refit is therefore excluded even though its features are already old, because its label is the return of the refit day itself. This label-date rule is the key leakage guard: checking feature dates alone would allow a label realized on the refit date into training. The walk-forward function asserts the rule at each refit, and a final check confirms it for all 319 refits. Every feature on day $t$ uses data up to day $t$ only; sentiment dated on calendar day $d$ is used only from the first trading day strictly after $d$, since posts may follow the market close (Friday to Sunday scores are averaged into Monday's row); the volatility adjustment uses $\hat\sigma_{t-1}$; and predictions are centred using only the members of the prediction date. Explicit checks confirm the period boundary: the last development prediction, on 30 December 2010, is scored on 31 December 2010, and the first test outcome, on 3 January 2011, uses the prediction made on 31 December 2010.

\subsection{Proposed Solution}
\label{sec:t3_solution}

\textit{Models.} We use three scikit-learn regressors~\cite{sklearn}. The Lasso~\cite{tibshirani1996} minimizes
$\frac{1}{2n}\sum(y-\hat y)^2+\alpha\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_1$,
which can set the weights of uninformative predictors to zero. The random forest~\cite{breiman2001} averages 100 regression trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample with splits chosen to reduce squared error; all five predictors are considered at each split. The MLP has one hidden layer of ReLU units and a linear output, and is trained on squared error with scikit-learn's L2 regularization parameter $\alpha=10^{-3}$, using the Adam optimizer with learning rate $10^{-3}$, mini-batches of 1,024 rows and a fixed number of epochs. The baselines are the 5-day reversal rule and 12-1 momentum, applied without fitting.

\textit{Hyperparameter tuning.} The grids were fixed before the compact run and contain 11 configurations: Lasso penalties $\alpha\in\{10^{-2},10^{-3},10^{-4}\}$; random forests with maximum depth $\in\{4,8\}$ and minimum leaf size $\in\{1000,200\}$; and MLPs with $\{16,32\}$ hidden units trained for $\{20,40\}$ epochs. Within each family, we select the configuration with the highest development Sharpe ratio pooled over 1990--2010, retaining the first configuration in the declared grid order in an exact tie. Sharpe ratios over 1990--1994, 1995--1999, 2000--2004 and 2005--2010 serve as diagnostics of stability. The selected configurations are a Lasso with $\alpha=10^{-4}$, a random forest of depth 8 with leaves of at least 200 rows, and an MLP with 32 hidden units trained for 40 epochs.

\textit{Smoothing and sentiment.} To lengthen the holding period, a fourth strategy applies an exponentially weighted average to the selected MLP's predictions, with a half-life of five trading days fixed before the compact run. On consecutive days with predictions, the update is
$s_{t,i}=(1-a)s_{t-1,i}+a\hat r_{t,i}$,
where $a=1-2^{-1/5}$. Sentiment begins in July 2009, leaving 2010 as the available pre-test evaluation year. We fit the selected Lasso with and without the sentiment predictors on identical training dates from July 2009 and compare them on 2010 outcomes. This comparison is exploratory and does not select a test strategy.

\subsection{Design Rationale}
\label{sec:t3_rationale}

\textit{Development and test periods.} The assignment specifies 2011--2013 as the test period. We begin development evaluation in 1990 to allow a long initial history for volatility estimation, feature construction and the first three-year training window. The 21-year development period spans different regimes, including the 2000--2002 crash and 2008. Selection maximizes pooled development Sharpe; sub-period results are inspected separately for stability. The assignment's rough Gaussian approximation gives a development Sharpe standard error of about 0.22.

\textit{Clipping and the clipped target.} Approximately 0.2\% of observed pre-2011 member adjusted returns exceed $\pm5$. Clipping leaves about 99.8\% unchanged while reducing the influence of extreme observations on squared-error training: an error of 30 contributes as much squared loss as 900 errors of 1. We clip next-day adjusted returns before demeaning them across prediction-day members, focusing the target on relative returns. Evaluation uses the original, unclipped adjusted returns.

\textit{Five features.} The 1-, 5- and 21-day returns represent short-term reversal at three horizons. Momentum represents the longer-term tendency of past winners to keep winning; it skips the most recent month so that it does not overlap with the reversal windows. The volatility level lets the nonlinear models adapt these relationships to a stock's risk. The predictors are only moderately correlated, whereas the earlier study described below found that adding sector-relative and smoothed variants mostly created near-duplicates. Five predictors keep the models explainable and limit the opportunities to fit spurious relationships, while ranks provide a common scale without fitting a scaler.

\textit{Hyperparameter grids.} Each configuration requires a full 21-year walk-forward evaluation, so we use small grids to limit computation and selection overfitting. The Lasso penalties are logarithmically spaced. The forest combines limited depth with large leaves to average noisy targets. The MLP uses one hidden layer, an L2 penalty and a fixed training budget of 20 or 40 epochs. Its penalty and learning rate are both fixed at $10^{-3}$ to limit the search. Training duration is tuned on development performance; no validation-based early stopping is used.

\textit{Refitting, window length and sample size.} Changing relationships motivate regular refitting on recent history. We refit every 252 trading days using the latest 756 eligible feature days, roughly three years and 375,000 member stock-days before filtering. Each fit samples at most 100,000 rows without replacement to bound computation, using deterministic seeds. The cadence and sample cap are fixed design choices; this compact study does not measure the performance cost of less frequent refitting or subsampling.

\textit{Overfitting through repeated testing.} We limited the core search to 11 configurations, declared before the compact run, with a single selection rule on the development period. This design followed an earlier, larger study run with an LLM agent, using 18 return predictors and additional sentiment variants. Its log contains 62 development experiments: 53 fitted-model runs, two baselines, two controls and five delayed-execution diagnostics. Its 2011--2013 results had been viewed before the compact design was fixed, so the test period here is not untouched. We therefore treat the compact results as indicative rather than as fresh, independent confirmation.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:t3_metrics}

\textit{P\&L and Sharpe ratio.} Following the assignment, the portfolio invests in each stock in proportion to its predicted adjusted return divided by its estimated volatility. Its profit-and-loss (P\&L) on trading day $d$, immediately after prediction day $t$, is
\begin{equation}
R_d=\sum_i \hat r_{t,i}\,\tilde r_{d,i},
\end{equation}
where the sum is over members of day $t$ with an observed adjusted outcome, the predictions $\hat r_{t,i}$ are centred across prediction-day members, and $\tilde r_{d,i}$ is the unclipped adjusted return. The annualized Sharpe ratio is
$\mathrm{SR}=\sqrt{252}\,\overline R/s_R$,
where $s_R$ is the sample standard deviation. No benchmark is subtracted because returns are already in excess of the risk-free rate. Unlike mean squared error, Sharpe measures return per unit of risk. Under the assignment's rough Gaussian approximation, its standard error is about $1/\sqrt{\tau}$ for $\tau$ years of data: approximately 0.58 for the three-year test period and 0.22 for the 21-year development period. These approximations do not account for repeated model selection or dependence over time. We also report Sharpe by sub-period and year, and plot cumulative P\&L to examine stability.

\textit{Rank correlation.} The information coefficient (IC) is the daily Spearman correlation between predicted and realized returns across stocks. It measures whether the predicted ordering is correct and is less sensitive to extreme magnitudes than Pearson correlation. Its cumulative sum shows whether ranking information persists over the evaluation period.

\textit{Holding period, market exposure and delay.} The average holding period $H_{av}$ applies the assignment's formula to each stock over pairs of consecutive days with valid predictions and observed adjusted outcomes, then averages across stocks. Missing days are not bridged. The market correlation $\rho_{\mathrm{mkt}}$ is the Pearson correlation of $R_d$ with the next-day raw return of an equal-weight portfolio of the prediction day's members. This diagnoses exposure to common market movements. The one-day-late Sharpe ratio applies each prediction one trading day later than intended. The assignment suggests gross Sharpe ratios of about 2 for one-day holding periods and 0.8 to 1 for three to five days as rough indicators of practical interest; these thresholds are not a transaction-cost model.

\textit{Development period.} More flexible configurations performed better: the forest's development Sharpe ratio rose from 4.04 (depth 4, leaves of 1,000) to 4.75 (depth 8, leaves of 200), and the MLP's from 3.47 (16 units, 20 epochs) to 3.96 (32 units, 40 epochs). The Lasso scored 2.6196 and 2.6248 for $\alpha=10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$, while $\alpha=10^{-2}$ set every coefficient to zero, giving constant predictions and an undefined Sharpe ratio after centring. The selected forest and MLP lie at the edge of their grids, which we kept as declared. Table~\ref{tab:t3_dev} compares the selected models with the baselines. The forest ranks above the MLP in every sub-period, and both outperform the Lasso and reversal rule. However, the Lasso falls below reversal in 2000--2004 and 2005--2010. Performance declines substantially between the first and last sub-periods: the forest falls from 8.83 to 2.08, and the Lasso from 5.27 to 1.04. Smoothing the MLP lengthens its holding period from 1.04 to 4.87 days and lowers its Sharpe ratio from 3.96 to 1.82. In the sentiment comparison, adding sentiment changed the 2010 Lasso Sharpe ratio from 0.61 to 0.59. This small exploratory difference over one year provides no convincing evidence of a benefit from sentiment.

\textit{Test period.} Table~\ref{tab:t3_test} and Fig.~\ref{fig:t3_test} report the results on 2011--2013. All three fitted models beat both baselines. The random forest reaches a gross Sharpe ratio of 3.19 and has positive annual Sharpe ratios in every test year (3.44, 3.45 and 2.80). The MLP reaches 2.34 but is less stable (3.08, 1.78 and 2.31), and the Lasso reaches 1.31. All three exceed their 2005--2010 level, whereas the reversal rule continues to decline (0.73, with a loss in 2012). The cumulative rank correlation of the forest and the MLP rises overall, with intervening declines (Fig.~\ref{fig:t3_test}, bottom), consistent with predictive information in their stock orderings over this sample. The two metrics do not always agree: momentum has the highest mean IC (0.019) but a Sharpe ratio of only 0.78. IC evaluates daily ordering, whereas P\&L depends on prediction magnitudes and realized return magnitudes; a higher average IC therefore need not imply a higher Sharpe ratio.

Three diagnostics temper these results. First, the fitted strategies change rapidly ($H_{av}\approx0.9$ days). The forest and MLP exceed the assignment's rough gross Sharpe benchmark of 2 for this horizon, while the Lasso does not; this does not establish profitability after costs. Second, the fitted strategies are sensitive to execution timing: one day late, the forest retains about half its Sharpe ratio (1.58), the MLP about a third (0.77) and the Lasso about two thirds (0.85), while the baseline Sharpe ratios change comparatively little. Third, centring predictions does not make the strategies market neutral: the fitted models' P\&L has correlations of 0.24 to 0.30 with the equal-weight market. The smoothed MLP holds positions for approximately 4.2 days and retains about three quarters of its Sharpe ratio when delayed (1.24 to 0.91). Its delayed score lies within the assignment's rough range of interest for that horizon, although its 2011 Sharpe ratio is only 0.31.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:t3_conclusion}

The results suggest small but persistent predictive information in past returns for next-day relative returns. The random forest captures this information best in the observed comparison, ahead of the MLP and Lasso; the short sentiment comparison shows no measurable benefit. Performance is sensitive to execution timing, the fitted models have short holding periods, and the test period had been viewed in an earlier study. The smoothed MLP offers a longer-holding candidate for further cost-aware evaluation, although practical profitability remains untested. Future work should evaluate on a genuinely later period, train on an execution-lagged target (the return from $t+1$ to $t+2$), include transaction costs in model selection, extend the grids beyond their edges under a rule fixed in advance, and revisit sentiment with a longer history.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ==================================================================
On Diabetes, regularized linear models implemented from scratch slightly improved on ordinary least squares on the selected split. ElasticNet shrank coefficients, while the proximal Lasso set several exactly to zero. On Digits, the MLP and random forest achieved 97.04\% and 96.48\% test accuracies, respectively, ahead of the linear SVM at 95.56\%. The hyperparameter studies showed sensitivity to regularization, learning rate, model capacity and training duration, although the observed curves alone do not establish every underlying cause. In Task~3, delayed execution substantially reduced the fitted models' Sharpe ratios, demonstrating the importance of evaluation timing. Improvements include selecting regression hyperparameters by cross-validation rather than inspecting test MSE, investigating mini-batch or adaptive optimization and validation-based early stopping for the scratch models, and evaluating standardized Digits features. For Task~3, future work should use an execution-lagged target and explicit transaction costs, with evaluation on a later period that has not previously been inspected.
